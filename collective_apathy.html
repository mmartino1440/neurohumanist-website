Our Collective Apathy to Individual Suffering

On wellness lectures, institutional responsibility, and the question of when the right time ever is

Michael Martino · June 2026

Medical and graduate schools love to talk about work-life balance. They'll tell you what you should be doing to take care of yourself — get enough sleep, exercise regularly, maintain social connections — which are things most of us know and would naturally do if the environment we were placed in allowed for it. The same institutions that dispense this advice, however, reliably fail to create an environment where following it is remotely feasible. This is a contradiction they seem content to live with.

During a lecture on wellness, I once asked the speaker directly: where do we draw the line between personal responsibility and institutional responsibility? The question wasn't adversarial. It came from a genuine recognition that our behavior is shaped by far more than the conscious choices we make in the moment — that environment, incentive structure, and the demands placed on us by the systems we inhabit are powerful determinants of how we actually live. Her answer was memorable: "These types of conversations don't tend to go well, because the people in power become very defensive, so it's best to just focus on what you can do personally."

Pardon my language, but I call bullshit.

The obsession with individual responsibility, when properly deployed — that is, limited and balanced by a realistic account of the other forces shaping behavior — can promote genuine self-reliance. What we have instead is individual responsibility weaponized: a rhetorical tool that absolves those with the power to make structural changes of any obligation to actually make them. There is wisdom in the idea that with great power comes great responsibility. It seems these institutions are happy to exert the former while disclaiming the latter. When words and actions contradict each other, the actions are the truth.

· · ·

Perhaps I'm being too sensitive, or holding too narrow a view. Let me test that.

These same institutions recognize, at least on paper, that graduate and medical students experience mental illness and burnout at rates significantly higher than the general population. As evidence of their commitment to student mental health, they point to free counseling services — a meaningful resource, in principle. What happens when you actually try to use them?

During my medical training, we spent time on the topic of intimate partner violence. For most of my adult life, I could discuss my own experiences in that area without much emotional difficulty — I'd learned to intellectualize it, to hold it at arm's length. I was entirely unprepared for what happened when we went through the clinical criteria for intimate partner violence: not personal stories, not graphic accounts, but the organizational framework itself, the diagnostic categories used to distinguish what counts as abuse from what doesn't. Something about seeing it laid out that way broke through whatever defenses I'd built. The emotional force of it surprised me. I sought counseling through the university.

My first therapist told me she very much wanted to help me, but that the volume of students seeking services made it unrealistic to meet with any frequency. I understood. I continued to see her periodically, working on other things. When she eventually left and I began with someone new — someone who had the availability to meet more regularly — this second therapist gently cautioned that confronting trauma was not a small endeavor, and that given the demands of my program, she wasn't sure this was a good time.

I passively agreed. I internalized the message: my mental wellbeing is not a priority right now, and should be deferred.

But if not now, when? When the unresolved trauma surfaces during patient rounds and I'm no longer able to contain it? When it shows up in my behavior in ways that require disciplinary action? Or never — as I keep it on the periphery of my attention by staying relentlessly busy, until some day, exhausted at the end of a long life, I realize I never once had the conditions to actually deal with it.

· · ·

I want to be clear that I'm doing my best to learn from and work through my own history. But I'm also aware that I'm unusually positioned to do so — my particular training has given me frameworks for understanding human behavior that most people don't have access to. That isn't credit to me. That's luck. What about the person in the same program, with the same history, who doesn't have that luck?

The harder question isn't whether I'll eventually be okay. It's whether any of this is necessary. Is the gap between what these institutions promise and what they deliver a timeless feature of how organizations work — the inevitable compromise between rhetoric and resource — or is it a specific product of our current cultural moment, one in which the market has become the final arbiter of what human suffering deserves attention? Have I not yet demonstrated sufficient value, in my commitment to science and medicine, to be afforded the chance to resolve my deepest source of personal pain? Or does that framing itself reveal something — that I've absorbed the very logic I'm critiquing, and started calculating the return on investment of my own healing?

I don't have a clean resolution here. What I have is a conviction that the conversation my wellness speaker refused to have — about the line between personal and institutional responsibility — is one we desperately need to start having, without the people in power getting defensive, and without the rest of us backing down just to avoid the discomfort. The suffering is real. The question of who is responsible for addressing it is not a conversation that should end because it makes administrators uncomfortable.

A note on personal narrative: we humans love stories, and my personal history as I've told it here is not a perfect recollection of events but a version that currently makes sense to me and serves the purpose of this essay. The facts are accurate; the framing is mine.
