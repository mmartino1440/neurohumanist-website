The Flaw in "Wonderfully Made"

On neurodevelopment, the myth of perfection, and who pays the price when the brain doesn't build itself cleanly

Michael Martino · June 2026

We are told we are wonderfully made. You'll hear it in graduation speeches, self-help books, religious texts, therapy offices. It's meant to be comforting — a reminder that each person has inherent value, that who you are is enough, that you are not a mistake. As a cultural message, it means well.

The problem is what it asks us to ignore about biology.

The brain does not build itself according to a perfect blueprint. It builds itself through a process that is, by any honest account, probabilistic, fragile, and prone to variation — and understanding that process has become one of the central preoccupations of my scientific life. When you look closely at how the human brain actually develops, the idea that we are perfectly made starts to look less like a truth and more like a wish. And wishes, when they harden into ideology, can cause real harm.

· · ·

Consider neural migration. During embryonic development, neurons are born deep in the brain and must travel long distances to reach their final positions in the cortex. This journey is guided by molecular signals — essentially a biological GPS — and it is not a sure thing. Cells receive ambiguous instructions. They encounter unexpected obstacles. They sometimes arrive in the wrong place, or don't arrive at all. The architecture that results from this migration is not a precise copy of some ideal plan. It's the outcome of millions of probabilistic events, each one slightly different from what the textbook predicts.

After migration comes synaptic pruning, which operates by a different but equally unintuitive logic. The developing brain overproduces connections — far more than it will ultimately use — and then, over years, carves away what isn't needed through a process of competitive selection. Think of a sculptor removing material to reveal the form inside the block. This pruning can go differently in different people. The timing, the extent, the targets — all of it varies. Some of that variation is benign. Some of it is associated with conditions like autism, schizophrenia, and ADHD, not because something went catastrophically wrong, but because a complex, multistep developmental process landed in a different place than the statistical average.

Given this level of complexity — the sheer number of molecular events that have to go approximately right for a typical brain to develop — variation is not an aberration. It is a mathematical inevitability. The question is not why some brains differ from the norm. It's why we're so determined to treat that difference as failure.

· · ·

A child with ADHD is not failing to try hard enough. Their prefrontal cortex — the region most implicated in sustained attention, impulse control, and executive function — develops on a different timeline and with a different connectivity profile than a neurotypical brain. That's not a moral deficiency. It's a developmental trajectory. But the message the world reliably delivers to that child is that they are not doing enough, that with more effort they could focus, that their difficulty sitting still or staying on task is a choice they're making — a character flaw, not a biological reality.

What that message does to a child is significant. By the time many people with neurodevelopmental differences reach adulthood, they have spent years internalizing the idea that their struggles are their own fault. Not because anyone sat them down and told them this explicitly, but because the systems around them were designed for a different brain, and the repeated experience of not fitting those systems reads — to a developing mind — as personal failure. The tyranny in this is that the person who has the least control over their neurodevelopment ends up bearing the full weight of the consequences.

The emotional toll compounds over time. Depression and anxiety in people with ADHD, autism, and other neurodevelopmental conditions are partly downstream of the conditions themselves — and partly downstream of spending years in an environment that kept communicating: something is wrong with you. Those are different problems with different implications, and conflating them has made treatment harder and more fraught than it needed to be.

· · ·

This isn't only a story about individuals. Our policies reflect our beliefs about human nature, and the belief that people are fundamentally responsible for their own neurological wiring has shaped how we build institutions in ways that are both costly and cruel.

If we believe that developmental differences are mostly failures of will or character, we build educational systems that sort children into those who "get it" and those who don't, and spend most of our energy trying to pull the latter category toward the former rather than asking what those students actually need to learn. We fund mental health services at a fraction of what we'd spend on a comparably prevalent physical illness, because we still, despite decades of evidence, treat psychiatric conditions as somehow more voluntary than other medical conditions. We design social safety nets with enormous holes and then express surprise when people fall through them.

A society that takes neurodevelopmental biology seriously would build differently. It would invest heavily in early intervention, because the developing brain is most responsive to support during the years when it's most actively forming. It would design educational environments for cognitive diversity rather than assuming diversity is the problem. It would ask, as the first question rather than the last, not "what is wrong with this person?" but "what does this person need to function and thrive?"

That shift — from blame to support, from deficit to design — doesn't require abandoning the idea that people have inherent worth. It requires taking that idea seriously enough to act on it. We are told we are wonderfully made. What I'd like to see is a world that is wonderfully designed — one where the inevitable variation in how brains build themselves doesn't determine whether someone gets to live a good life. That would be something worth saying at a graduation speech.
