The Signal in the Noise

Why we mistake behavior for character, and what it costs us

Michael Martino · June 2026

Picture a patient — call her Maria — who calls the clinic three or four times a week. She shows up late to appointments, doesn't follow the medication schedule, and occasionally refuses recommended procedures. In the shorthand of a busy clinic, she's a difficult patient. The chart reflects this. Future providers approach her accordingly.

Now imagine asking a different question: not "what kind of person is she?" but "what is her behavior trying to tell us?"

This is not a minor shift in framing. It is, I'd argue, one of the most consequential distinctions in medicine — and well beyond it. In training, we are taught to observe behavior carefully. We are taught far less often to resist the instinct to read that behavior as character. Yet the difference between those two interpretations determines almost everything about how we respond.

· · ·

The place to start is biology. Behavior, at its most fundamental level, is homeostatic — it exists to maintain balance. When you pull your hand from a hot stove, that's not weakness; it's your nervous system doing exactly what it was designed to do. When you eat when you're hungry, that's not gluttony; it's the restoration of energy balance. These behaviors look like choices but they're better understood as responses, generated by systems that are tracking deficits and driving toward correction.

The principle scales. The more deprived the system, the more intense the drive to correct. If you've ever tried intermittent fasting, you know this: a meal you'd normally eat without much thought can, after a long fast, feel almost transcendently satisfying. The food didn't change. What changed was the depth of the deficit. Deprivation doesn't eliminate the drive — it amplifies it.

Social connection operates by the same rules. There is now substantial evidence that the need for belonging and social validation is not a preference or a personality trait. It's a biological need, tracked by the same systems that track hunger and pain. When it's consistently unmet, those systems generate the same kind of escalating, urgent, difficult-to-override behavior that starvation generates. A person who is chronically isolated and makes what looks like erratic social decisions isn't demonstrating a character flaw. They're demonstrating what sustained deprivation of a fundamental need actually looks like from the outside.

· · ·

Sam Altman has said publicly that one of the things he finds most striking about how people use AI models is the degree to which they're seeking emotional connection. Not productivity tools. Not information retrieval. Something that listens, responds, and doesn't leave. An increasing number of people have reported developing something like romantic attachment to their AI chatbots. This has been met with a combination of mockery and alarm, which I think misses the point entirely.

That behavior is a signal. It's telling us something about the scale of unmet need for genuine human connection in contemporary life — need so intense that the brain will route its social drives toward whatever is most reliably accessible, even if that thing is a language model that doesn't experience anything. You don't find that alarming because the people are confused about what a chatbot is. You find it alarming because the alternative — actual human connection — has apparently become inaccessible enough that a significant number of people have stopped expecting to find it.

Maria, who calls the clinic four times a week, might be telling us the same thing. Not that she's difficult, but that something in her environment is insufficient, and her behavior is the most visible symptom of that insufficiency.

· · ·

The clinical stakes here are real. Two doctors who interpret the same patient differently will ask entirely different questions. A doctor who reads behavior as character will reach for the medication checklist, add a note to the chart about noncompliance, and become increasingly frustrated. A doctor who reads behavior as signal will ask about what's happening at home, whether there's anyone she can rely on, what she's actually afraid of. These are not softer or less rigorous questions. They are more likely to surface the information that actually explains what's happening.

This extends to psychiatry in particular, where the stakes of misreading behavior as character are highest. A patient's distrust of the treatment system, however inconvenient it is to manage, is often a rational response to experiences that gave them very good reasons not to trust it. Labeling that distrust as a symptom rather than a signal doesn't just fail the patient — it often deepens the very problem it's meant to address.

· · ·

The broader implication is one I keep returning to: the stories we tell about why people behave the way they do are not neutral. They have downstream consequences for policy, for institutional design, for how we allocate resources and responsibility.

If we believe that behavior reflects character — that struggling people are fundamentally different from people who aren't struggling, that their difficulties are primarily expressions of who they are rather than what they've been through — then the logical response to social problems is punishment and correction. We build systems that identify the flawed individual and attempt to fix or contain them.

If we believe that behavior is often a signal — that it reflects what's available, what's been denied, what systems of support are or aren't in place — then the response is different. We build systems that ask what people actually need. We address the deficits that generate the behaviors we're trying to change, rather than trying to extinguish the behaviors while leaving the deficits in place.

Maria, who everyone agrees is difficult, might be the most coherent communicator in the room. She's been trying to tell us something for a long time. The question is whether we have learned to listen for signals, or whether we're still just sorting through the noise looking for someone to blame.
