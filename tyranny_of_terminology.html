The Tyranny of Terminology

When Science Forgets How to Talk

Michael Martino · June 2026

I remember sitting in a lab meeting early in my scientific career, watching two brilliant researchers nearly come to blows over the cause of obesity. One maintained it was fundamentally hormonal — insulin resistance, cortisol dysregulation, leptin signaling gone haywire. The other was equally convinced it was purely caloric: eat less, move more, full stop. The argument had the heat of an old grudge, the kind that breeds opposing grant applications and carefully worded critiques buried in peer review.

What struck me, as the newcomer in the room, was that both of them were right. They were describing the same biological reality from opposite ends of a very large elephant — and neither seemed to notice.

That was my introduction to what I've come to think of as the Tyranny of Terminology.

· · ·

Scientific jargon begins as a precision tool, and that precision genuinely matters. When researchers in a field share a common vocabulary, they can communicate enormous amounts of information quickly, without talking past each other. The problem is that the same precision that makes communication efficient within a field makes it nearly impossible across fields. The vocabulary stops serving science and starts serving scientists — specifically the ones who happen to own the terms.

This wouldn't be so damaging if it were just an inconvenience. But jargon, in the current academic system, has become something more than a communication shortcut. It's become a mechanism of territorial defense.

Here's why. The incentive structure of modern science demands novelty. To secure funding, earn tenure, and survive the publish-or-perish gauntlet, researchers must frame their work as new, distinct, and significant. The easiest way to establish novelty is to introduce new terminology, or to insist on a narrow conceptual framework — even when the underlying biological mechanism you're describing is functionally identical to something another field has been studying for decades. You don't claim you've discovered the same thing. You claim you've discovered something different, because your language is different. And the grant review panel, drawn largely from within your own silo, doesn't notice the overlap because they haven't read the other literature either.

The result is fields that talk mostly to themselves. The feud in that lab meeting was not about data. Both researchers had data. It was about who got to define the causal story — and by extension, who got the grant money and the journal covers.

· · ·

The cost of this isn't just wasted funding or academic embarrassment. When fields fail to talk to each other, complex problems stay unsolved longer than they need to. Obesity doesn't care whether you call it a hormonal disorder or a caloric imbalance. Chronic pain doesn't submit itself for classification before it decides which specialty to live in. Mental illness doesn't wait for the DSM to catch up with lived experience before it starts ruining people's lives.

The failure to synthesize isn't a failure of intelligence — the people in that lab meeting were genuinely brilliant. It's a failure of what you might call organizational intelligence: the collective inability to see the unified story that the data are telling, across the boundaries of disciplines, when no one is tasked with looking across those boundaries in the first place. When every lab is incentivized to dig deeper into one well rather than build bridges between wells, we get a scientific literature that is extraordinarily precise in its pieces and incoherent as a whole.

The field that made this clearest to me is computational psychiatry, which sits at the intersection of neuroscience, clinical medicine, and mathematical modeling. Working across those domains, you quickly realize that a "prediction error" in computational terms, a "maladaptive belief" in cognitive-behavioral terms, and a "disrupted reward signal" in neurobiological terms can describe the same moment in a patient's brain. The same thing. Described three times, in three separate vocabularies, with three distinct literatures — few of which cite each other, and none of which have agreed on a common unit of measurement.

If this seems like an academic problem, it doesn't stay one. The average patient with a serious mental health condition sees multiple providers across multiple specialties, each operating from a different conceptual framework, often reaching different conclusions from the same clinical picture. The fragmentation of knowledge across disciplines fragments care, too. The patient ends up in the gap between the wells.

· · ·

What's the antidote? I don't think it's training more scientists to learn more vocabulary, though that helps at the margins. The deeper problem is structural: we need to value a different kind of mind.

The researcher who digs deeply into one well is easy to evaluate. Their outputs fit neatly into existing categories. Their grant applications map onto established funding priorities. The researcher who builds bridges between wells — who reads across fields, translates between dialects, and notices when two distant literatures are converging on the same truth — is much harder to evaluate, and much harder to fund. The system pushes people away from exactly the work that matters most when problems are complex.

Some of the most productive intellectual moves in science happen when someone wanders into a field they didn't train in and notices a pattern the insiders couldn't see because they were too close to it. Darwin wasn't a geneticist. Ramón y Cajal trained as an artist before he drew the nervous system into coherence. The outsider perspective isn't a weakness to be remediated. It's often the only vantage point from which the whole picture becomes visible.

What I'm arguing for is a scientific culture that treats synthesis as a legitimate and valuable form of scholarship — not a consolation prize for people who couldn't specialize deeply enough. The most consequential problems in medicine and mental health cannot be solved from within any single disciplinary trench. They require people who are genuinely fluent across domains, who are more interested in the truth than in who gets to name it.

The two scientists in that lab meeting were never going to resolve their argument on those terms. The argument was never really about the science. But somewhere outside that room, a patient was waiting for us to figure it out — and probably still is.
